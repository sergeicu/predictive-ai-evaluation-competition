# Literature overview: predicting per-item LLM correctness without running the model

*For the Predictive AI Evaluation Competition (PAIEC), NeurIPS 2026. Researched 2026-10-20.*

Legend: **[S]** = survey/position/overview; **[P]** = primary method/empirical paper. **Verified** = abstract page or proceedings page fetched/confirmed during research; **(unverified)** = bibliographic details from secondary sources only.

## 0. The competition itself (verified from https://aims.stanford.edu/competition)

- Task: for each (subject, item) pair output P(correct) in [0,1] without running the subject. Subject = model × harness × harness version × reasoning effort. Item = benchmark question with `item_content`, `item_features`, `interactors`, `benchmark_id`.
- Benchmark-level cold start: test benchmarks are a private pool, randomly assigned before curation. Each subject–benchmark pair (≥80 items, split 50/50 into acquisition and evaluation pools) is scored at 0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 31 revealed labels; optional `labeling.py` chooses which acquisition items to reveal (default random).
- Metric: **Brier ALC** = normalized area under the Brier curve on a log2(1+n) axis with weights 0.1/0.2/0.2/0.2/0.2/0.1; ECE reported as diagnostic only. Organizers: S. Truong, O. Salaudeen, N. Truong, S. Wang, A. Wang, L. Guerdan, Z. Xiao, X. Xie, N. Haber, S. Koyejo.
- Baselines (https://github.com/aims-foundations/paiec_baseline): "Empirical Mean" (mean of revealed labels, 0.5 at n=0), "Bayesian Linguistic Evaluator" (LLM retrieves public evidence and updates an explicit belief state; uncertainty-sampling acquisition), and a hybrid. Training data: `aims-foundations/measurement-db` on HF (≈249 benchmark migrations, item-level responses; cite as N. Truong, S. T. Truong, S. Koyejo 2026, "The AI Measurement Data Bank").
- A public participant repo (https://github.com/paiksca/paiec-2026) reports a hierarchical logistic model (benchmark-level prior from ridge regression on item text + subject features, subject ability, subject×benchmark deviation) reaching ≈0.21 Brier-ALC in grouped CV vs 0.2125 for a smoothed label mean, and notes the text-feature prior only helps once ~80 training benchmarks are available. Treat as anecdotal.

## 1. Evaluation science / measurement-theory framing

1. **[S] Measurement to Meaning: A Validity-Centered Framework for AI Evaluation** — Salaudeen, Reuel, Ahmed, …, A. Wang, …, Koyejo (2025), arXiv:2505.10573, NeurIPS 2025 workshop. Verified. Imports the psychometric validity taxonomy (construct/content/criterion, nomological network) and argues evaluation claims must be matched to the evidence a benchmark can provide. Relevant because PAIEC is explicitly a "measurement science" exercise: item-level predictability is a form of validity evidence about what a benchmark measures.
2. **[S] Position: Evaluating Generative AI Systems Is a Social Science Measurement Challenge** — Wallach, Desai, Cooper, A. Wang, … Jacobs (ICML 2025), arXiv:2502.00561. Verified. Four-level framework (background concept → systematized concept → measurement instrument → instance measurement). Conceptual grounding for treating items as instruments and benchmarks as (imperfect) operationalizations.
3. **[S] AI Evaluation Should Require Standardized Item-Level Data Releases** (v1 title: "Position: Science of AI Evaluation Requires Item-level Benchmark Data") — H. Jiang, S. Zhang, D. Zhu, Y. Bai, S. T. Truong, X. Yi, Koyejo, X. Xie, Z. Xiao (2026), arXiv:2604.03244. Verified. Argues item-level responses (content, conditions, outputs, statistics) are infrastructure; releases OpenEval (~10.7M responses, ~160k items). Directly motivates measurement-db and PAIEC's data model.
4. **[S] Evaluating Evaluation Metrics: A Framework for Analyzing NLG Evaluation Metrics using Measurement Theory (MetricEval)** — Z. Xiao, S. Zhang, V. Lai, Q. V. Liao (EMNLP 2023), arXiv:2305.14889. Verified. Formalizes reliability/validity of metrics via classical test theory.
5. **[P] Validating LLM-as-a-Judge Systems under Rating Indeterminacy** — Guerdan et al. (NeurIPS 2025), arXiv:2503.05965. Verified. Shows naive validation can pick judges up to 34% worse; relevant to the fact that many PAIEC "responses" are themselves LLM-graded.
6. **[P] What AI Benchmarks Actually Measure: Convergent and Discriminant Validity across 56 benchmarks** — Desai, S. T. Truong, Wallach, …, Koyejo, A. Wang (2026), arXiv:2609.08812. Verified. Correlates model rankings and item-level IRT across 56 benchmarks × 53 models; finds same-concept and different-concept benchmarks often correlate similarly, and design features (score format) can dominate. Implication: cross-benchmark transfer of ability is real but concept labels are a weak guide to which benchmarks share variance.
7. **[S] Measuring What Matters: Construct Validity in LLM Benchmarks** — Bean et al. (NeurIPS 2025), arXiv:2511.04703. Verified. Systematic review of 445 benchmarks; checklist of validity failures (useful for engineering item features such as "reuses an exam", "MCQ vs free-form").
8. **[S] Rethink Reporting of Evaluation Results in AI** — Burnell, Schellaert, Burden, …, Hernández-Orallo (Science 380:136–138, 2023). Verified. Argues aggregate metrics hide instance-level behaviour and calls for instance-level result release. Intellectual precursor to the assessor line (section 4).
9. **[S] Predictable Artificial Intelligence** — L. Zhou, Hernández-Orallo et al. (2023; Artificial Intelligence vol. 353, 2026), arXiv:2310.06167. Verified. Formalizes "predictability" of AI validity indicators, what is predicted and by whom (assessors), and the predictability–performance trade-off. The closest thing to a position paper for PAIEC's exact problem.
10. **[S] Quantifying construct validity in LLM evaluations** — R. O. Kearns (2026 thesis), arXiv:2602.15532. Verified. "Structured capabilities model" combining scaling-law scale → latent capabilities → observed scores with measurement error; outperforms pure factor models and pure scaling laws on OOD benchmark prediction (Open LLM Leaderboard). A useful template for a subject-side prior from release date/size.
11. **[S] AACL 2026 Tutorial: Measurement Science for LM Evaluation** (Koyejo lab), https://aimslab.stanford.edu/tutorial. Verified page exists; bibliography lists amortized IRT, Fantastic Bugs, multigroup IRT for multilingual safety.

## 2. Item Response Theory, adaptive testing, and benchmark compression

12. **[P] Building an Evaluation Scale using Item Response Theory** — Lalor, Wu, Yu (EMNLP 2016), arXiv:1605.08889. Verified. First application of IRT to NLP test sets (RTE). Follow-up **[P] IRT with Artificial Crowds** — Lalor, Wu, Yu (EMNLP 2019), arXiv:1908.11421 (verified): fit IRT from an ensemble of models instead of humans; machine- and human-derived difficulties correlate medium-to-large. Software: py-irt.
13. **[P] Item Response Theory in AI: analysing ML classifiers at the instance level** — Martínez-Plumed, Prudêncio, Martínez-Usó, Hernández-Orallo (Artificial Intelligence 271:18–42, 2019). Verified (repository metadata). Treats instances as items and classifiers as respondents; relates IRT difficulty/discrimination/guessing to instance-hardness measures and introduces classifier characteristic curves.
14. **[P] Evaluation Examples Are Not Equally Informative** — Rodriguez, Barrow, Hoyle, Lalor, Jia, Boyd-Graber (ACL 2021). Verified. Bayesian IRT leaderboard; shows IRT ranks are more reliable than accuracy, flags annotation errors, and identifies informative items.
15. **[P] Comparing Test Sets with Item Response Theory** — Vania et al. (ACL 2021), arXiv:2106.00840. Verified. 29 datasets × 18 models; identifies saturated vs discriminative test sets.
16. **[P] Efficiently Measuring the Cognitive Ability of LLMs: An Adaptive Testing Perspective** — Y. Zhuang et al. (2023; later reframed as "Position: AI Evaluation Should Learn from How We Test Humans"), arXiv:2306.10512. Verified. Early CAT-for-LLMs; shows adaptive item selection yields ability estimates with far fewer items.
17. **[P] tinyBenchmarks: evaluating LLMs with fewer examples** — Maia Polo, Weber, Choshen, Sun, Xu, Yurochkin (ICML 2024, PMLR 235), arXiv:2402.14992. Verified. IRT fit on leaderboard data + anchor-point selection; **100 curated items per scenario give ~2% MAE on full-benchmark accuracy** (IRT++ within 1.9% on MMLU for new models). The canonical "how few items" result at the aggregate level.
18. **[P] Anchor Points: Benchmarking Models with Much Fewer Examples** — Vivek, Ethayarajh, Yang, Kiela (EACL 2024), arXiv:2309.08638. Verified. Model confidences on item pairs are highly correlated across models; **1–30 anchor points rank 87 model-prompt pairs better than uniform sampling, and ~a dozen anchors estimate per-example class predictions on all other points with low error**. Directly relevant to the 7–31 label regime.
19. **[P] metabench — A Sparse Benchmark of Reasoning and Knowledge** — Kipnis, Voudouris, Schulze Buschoff, Schulz (ICLR 2025), arXiv:2407.12844. Verified. From >5000 LLMs × 6 benchmarks (28,632 items), distils <3% of items; reconstructs individual benchmark scores at ≈1.24% RMSE and total score at 0.58% RMSE; a single common factor correlates r≈0.94 with total score.
20. **[P] Efficient Benchmarking (of Language Models)** — Perlitz et al. (NAACL 2024), arXiv:2308.11696. Verified. Introduces DIoR reliability measure and Flash-HELM; ~100× compute reduction with minimal rank loss. Companion **[P] Benchmark Agreement Testing Done Right (BenchBench)** — Perlitz et al. (2024/NeurIPS 2025 D&B), arXiv:2407.13696 (verified): methodological choices strongly affect measured agreement between benchmarks; relevant to deciding which training benchmarks "transfer" to a cold-start test benchmark.
21. **[P] Reliable and Efficient Amortized Model-based Evaluation** — S. T. Truong, Y. Tu, P. Liang, B. Li, S. Koyejo (ICML 2025), arXiv:2503.13335. Verified (full text). Rasch (1PL) model over 22 benchmarks × 172 models (2PL/3PL did not help with few test-takers). **Amortizes item difficulty with a linear head on frozen Llama-3-8B embeddings trained by response likelihood**, so unseen items get difficulty without calibration; Fisher-information adaptive testing cuts items by 53% on average (up to 86%) to reach 0.95 reliability (31 queries suffice on AIRBench); a FLOP-based ability predictor reaches AUC 0.82 on unseen models×questions. The organizers' own closest method; note it does not explicitly test a held-out benchmark.
22. **[P] Fluid Language Model Benchmarking** — Hofmann et al. (COLM 2025), arXiv:2509.11106. Verified (full text). 2PL IRT fit by MCMC with hierarchical priors on 102 pretrained LMs (ARC-C, GSM8K, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA, WinoGrande); MAP ability for new LMs; Fisher-info item selection with dynamic stopping. **With 10 items, Fluid matches validity (rank distance 10.1 vs 20.0 random) and halves variance; 50 items beat full-benchmark accuracy on validity/variance/saturation.** Dynamic stopping needed ~20–80 items depending on checkpoint. Does not evaluate per-item correctness prediction.
23. **[P] Adaptive Testing for LLM Evaluation (ATLAS)** — P. Li et al. (ICML 2026 spotlight), arXiv:2511.04689. Verified. IRT + Fisher-information CAT over >3000 models, 5 benchmarks; up to 90% fewer items (41 items reproduce whole-bank ability on HellaSwag, MAE 0.157); 23–31% of models shift >10 ranks when ranked by θ vs accuracy.
24. **[P] Lost in Benchmarks? PSN-IRT** — H. Zhou et al. (AAAI 2026 oral), arXiv:2505.15055. Verified. Richer-parameter IRT via pseudo-Siamese network on 11 benchmarks / 41,871 items; documents widespread low measurement quality of items.
25. **[P] Item Response Scaling Laws (IRSL / Beta-IRT)** — S. T. Truong, Y. Tu, R. Schaeffer, S. Koyejo (2026), arXiv:2606.07616. Verified. Factorizes model ability × item characteristics on probabilistic responses; 6,612 checkpoints × 37,682 questions × 10 benchmarks; after one-time calibration, **50 questions per benchmark suffice** and abilities transfer to other benchmarks measuring the same objective.
26. **[P] LLM Evaluation on Unseen Questions: Contextual Multidimensional IRT** — E. Shang, W. Tang, Y. He (2026), arXiv:2608.22295. Verified. Multidimensional IRT in which question embeddings inform item parameters; improves prediction on unseen items within a scenario, multidimensional > unidimensional, **but generalization across scenarios (≈ new benchmarks) does not reliably carry over**: a direct warning about benchmark-level cold start.
27. **[P] From Text to Parameters: Predicting Item Parameters from Embedding Regularization** — S.-T. Chen, J. Chen (2026), arXiv:2607.07141. Verified. Educational item banks (EEDI math, BEA 2024 medical): text embeddings recover difficulty with repeated-CV R²≈0.53 (~57% of the reliability ceiling); discrimination and guessing are much less predictable, largely because their targets are unreliable. Caution: single splits inflate R² by 0.10–0.15.
28. **[P] Easy2Hard-Bench** — M. Ding et al. (NeurIPS 2024 D&B), arXiv:2409.18433. Verified. IRT/Glicko-2 difficulty labels from human+LLM leaderboards across math, code, chess, reasoning; useful as an external difficulty feature source.
29. **[P] Efficient multi-prompt evaluation of LLMs (PromptEval)** — Maia Polo et al. (NeurIPS 2024), arXiv:2405.17202. Verified. IRT-style model over prompt templates × items estimates performance distribution over 100 templates for the cost of ~2 evaluations. Relevant because "harness" differences in PAIEC subjects are partly prompt-template differences.
30. **[P] Efficient Evaluation of LLMs via Collaborative Filtering** — X.-X. Zhong, C. Yi, H.-J. Ye (2025), arXiv:2504.08781. Verified. LLMs-as-users / items-as-items; two-stage instance selection then rating-prediction for unobserved items.
31. **[P] Active Evaluation Acquisition for Efficient LLM Benchmarking** — Y. Li, J. Ma, Ballesteros, Benajiba, Horwood (ICML 2025), arXiv:2410.05952. Verified. Learns dependencies among test items and an RL acquisition policy to pick which items to run so the rest can be predicted, the same structure as PAIEC's `labeling.py`.
32. **[P] Scales++: Compute-efficient evaluation subset selection with cognitive-scales embeddings** — Bean, Seedat, Chen, Schwarz (2025), arXiv:2510.26384. Verified. Item-centric selection by cognitive demands (ADeLe-style) rather than past model behaviour; explicitly targets the **cold-start** case for new benchmarks; 0.5% subset predicts Open LLM Leaderboard scores at 2.9% MAE with 18× lower selection cost.
33. **[P] Efficient Benchmarking Is Just Feature Selection and Multiple Regression** — Bowyer, Locatelli, Cao (2026), arXiv:2605.25773. Verified. Reframes subset selection as mRMR feature selection + kernel ridge regression; beats IRT/clustering methods except in very data-poor settings.
34. **[P] Cer-Eval** (Xu, Wang, Li, Chen 2025, arXiv:2505.03814) and **CELEUS** (2026, arXiv:2606.20820). Verified via search. Certifiable, anytime-valid evaluation with 20–60% fewer samples; relevant to uncertainty-aware stopping.
35. **[S] Are We Testing Too Much? A Survey of Benchmark Compression** — G. Ling et al. (2026), arXiv:2610.18248. Verified. 119 papers; taxonomy: within-benchmark, across-suite, adaptive compression, plus redundancy/reliability. Also curated list: https://github.com/MilkThink-Lab/Awesome-Benchmark-Compression.
36. **[P] Fantastic Bugs and Where to Find Them in AI Benchmarks** — S. T. Truong et al. (NeurIPS 2025 D&B), arXiv:2511.16842. Verified. IRT-style item statistics flag invalid items with up to 84% precision across 9 benchmarks; relevant because mislabelled items cap achievable Brier.

## 3. Aggregate performance prediction and predictability

37. **[P] How Predictable Are LLM Capabilities? A Case Study on BIG-bench** — Q. Ye, H. Fu, X. Ren, R. Jia (Findings of EMNLP 2023), arXiv:2305.14947. Verified. MLP over (model family, size, task, #shots) predicts task-level BIG-bench scores with R²>95% on random splits; also finds a "small-bench" subset as informative as BBH for new model families. Shows strong low-rank structure in the model×task matrix.
38. **[P] How predictable is language model benchmark performance?** — D. Owen (Epoch, 2024), arXiv:2401.04757. Verified. Compute-scaling extrapolation: ~6 pp error for BBH aggregate but ~18 pp for individual tasks over one OOM of compute. Aggregate predictability ≫ task-level predictability.
39. **[P] Observational Scaling Laws and the Predictability of LM Performance** — Ruan, Maddison, Hashimoto (NeurIPS 2024 spotlight), arXiv:2405.10938. Verified. ~100 public models; PCA of standardized benchmark scores yields a low-dimensional "principal capability" space with family-specific compute efficiency; predicts emergent and agentic performance from cheap benchmarks. Foundation for subject embeddings from other benchmarks.
40. **[P] Collaborative Performance Prediction for LLMs (CPP)** — Q. Zhang, F. Lyu, X. Liu, C. Ma (EMNLP 2024), arXiv:2407.01300. Verified. Matrix-factorization/CF over models × tasks with model and task design features as side information; beats scaling laws for scaled-up models and supports cold-start via side features. The task-level analogue of PAIEC.
41. **[P] Sloth: scaling laws for LLM skills** — Maia Polo, Somerstep, Choshen, Sun, Yurochkin (NeurIPS 2025), arXiv:2412.06540. Verified. Low-dimensional latent skills shared across families, with one model per family sufficient to identify family efficiency; 12 benchmarks from Open LLM Leaderboard.
42. **[P] EmbedLLM: Learning Compact Representations of LLMs** — Zhuang, Wu, Wen, Li, Jiao, Ramchandran (ICLR 2025), arXiv:2410.02223. Verified. Encoder–decoder learns model embeddings by reconstructing per-question correctness; embeddings forecast benchmark performance and route queries. Closest public work to "subject embedding + item embedding → P(correct)".
43. **[P] Predicting Emergent Capabilities by Finetuning** — Snell, Wallace, Klein, Levine (COLM 2024), arXiv:2411.16035. Verified. Fine-tuning shifts the emergence point; predicts emergence up to ~4× compute ahead. Relevant for release-date/scale priors at n=0.
44. **[P] Larger and more instructable language models become less reliable** — L. Zhou, Schellaert, Martínez-Plumed, Moros-Daval, Ferri, Hernández-Orallo (Nature 634:61–68, 2024), DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07930-y. Verified. Human-perceived difficulty is only loosely concordant with model failure; no "safe zone" of easy items; error patterns shift from avoidance to confident wrong answers with scale/instruction tuning. Implication: human-difficulty features alone give limited per-item predictability.

## 4. Per-instance correctness prediction ("assessors")

45. **[S/P] Training on the Test Set: Mapping the System-Problem Space in AI** — Hernández-Orallo, Schellaert, Martínez-Plumed (AAAI 2022 Blue Sky), DOI 10.1609/aaai.v36i11.21487. Verified. Introduces the **assessor** R̂(r | π, μ): a conditional estimator trained on test results that predicts a system's outcome on a new problem. Origin of the framing PAIEC uses.
46. **[P] Your Prompt is My Command: Human-centred generality of multimodal models** — Schellaert, Martínez-Plumed, …, Hernández-Orallo (JAIR 77, 2023), DOI 10.1613/jair.1.14157. Verified. Argues generality/predictability must be assessed relative to user prompting distributions; less central than the above.
47. **[P] 100 instances is all you need** — Pacchiardi, Cheke, Hernández-Orallo (2024), arXiv:2409.03563. Verified (full text). Generic assessor = XGBoost/LR on [new model's binary success vector on ~100 reference items ‖ OpenAI text-embedding-3-large of the instance], trained over 14 OpenAI models × 37,529 instances (KindsOfReasoning) and 30 models × 4,285 (HELM-Lite). **In-distribution it matches model-specific assessors trained on everything; performance saturates at ~100 reference instances; random reference selection ≈ k-means/factor-analysis selection; OOD (new datasets) all methods degrade and no method wins.** This is the single closest prior design to PAIEC's few-shot regime.
48. **[P] PredictaBoard: Benchmarking LLM Score Predictability** — Pacchiardi, Voudouris, Slater, Martínez-Plumed, Hernández-Orallo, Zhou, Schellaert (Findings of ACL 2025), arXiv:2502.14445. Verified (full text). 41 LLMs × 12 prompt-only assessors (OpenAI/Word2Vec/FastText/n-gram × LR-l1/LR-l2/XGBoost) on MMLU-Pro (ID) and BBH (OOD). **Most pairs barely beat random AUROC / constant-Brier baselines; best reach AUROC≈0.7; predictability is higher for high-accuracy models; XGBoost is poorly calibrated; OOD is markedly worse and ID winners do not transfer.** Metric: accuracy–rejection curve / predictably-valid region. Code: github.com/Kinds-of-Intelligence-CFI/PredictaBoard.
49. **[P] General Scales Unlock AI Evaluation with Explanatory and Predictive Power (ADeLe)** — L. Zhou, Hernández-Orallo et al. (2025; Nature 652:58–67, 2026), arXiv:2503.06378. Verified (full text). GPT-4o rates each of 16,108 instances (63 tasks, 20 benchmarks) on 18 demand dimensions (0–5); random forest on demand profile predicts per-instance success for 15 LLMs. **AUROC: ID 0.839 (ECE 0.011), new-task OOD 0.810, new-benchmark OOD 0.747 — vs GloVe-embedding RF 0.805/0.740/0.480 and fine-tuned LLaMA 0.840/0.788/0.692.** The strongest published evidence that interpretable item-demand features transfer to unseen benchmarks where raw embeddings collapse.
50. **[P] What should an AI assessor optimise for?** — Romero-Alvarado, Martínez-Plumed, Hernández-Orallo (2025), arXiv:2502.00365. Verified. Training assessors on a transformed loss (e.g., log-score) and mapping back can beat training on the target metric; relevant to Brier-targeted training.
51. **[P] Generalized Correctness Models (GCM)** — H. Xiao, Patil, Lee, Stengel-Eskin, Bansal (ICML 2026), arXiv:2509.24988. Verified. An LLM predicting its own correctness is no better than an unrelated LLM; a Qwen3-8B trained to predict many models' correctness from question (+ history) transfers across families (≥3% better than per-model CMs, ECE<0.03 without post-hoc calibration) on MMLU/TriviaQA.
52. **[P] No Answer Needed: Predicting LLM answer accuracy from question-only linear probes** — Moreno Cencerrado, …, Pacchiardi (ICLR 2026 workshop), arXiv:2509.10625. Verified. Pre-generation activation probes beat black-box embedding assessors and verbalised confidence; OOD knowledge transfer is good, math transfer weaker. Note: PAIEC forbids running the subject, but probes of a *different* open model are allowed features.
53. **[P] LLMs Encode Their Failures: Predicting Success from Pre-Generation Activations** — Lugoloobi, Foster, Bankes, Russell (COLM 2026), arXiv:2602.09924. Verified. Model-specific difficulty diverges from human difficulty (E2H-AMC), gap grows with extended reasoning; routing with probes cuts cost up to 70%.
54. **[P] IRT-Router** — W. Song et al. (ACL 2025), arXiv:2506.01048. Verified. Routing framed as IRT (LLMs = test-takers, queries = items) with semantic-similarity warm-up for new queries, i.e., item cold-start via embeddings. The LLM-routing literature generally (RouterBench, "Fly-Swat or Cannon?"; **unverified, not retrieved**) is an adjacent source of per-query correctness predictors.
55. **[P] Zhou et al. Nature 2024** (entry 44) also belongs here for its per-instance difficulty-concordance analysis.

Not found: papers named "PRECOG" or "Lost in the Haystack/capability vectors" on this topic could not be verified; treat as non-existent unless a specific reference is in hand.

## 5. Cold start, few-shot adaptation, active and label-efficient evaluation

56. **[P] Active Testing: Sample-Efficient Model Evaluation** — Kossen, Farquhar, Gal, Rainforth (ICML 2021), arXiv:2103.05331. Verified. Importance-weighted acquisition of test labels with bias correction; unbiased low-variance accuracy estimates from few labels.
57. **[P] Active Surrogate Estimators** — Kossen, Farquhar, Gal, Rainforth (NeurIPS 2022), arXiv:2202.06881. Verified. Replace Monte Carlo with a surrogate that interpolates errors on unlabeled points; acquisition (XWED/XWING) tailored to estimation. Conceptually identical to "predict unlabeled items from labeled ones," but for accuracy estimation rather than per-item Brier.
58. **[P] Prediction-Powered Inference** — Angelopoulos, Bates, Fannjiang, Jordan, Zrnic (Science 382:669–674, 2023), arXiv:2301.09633. Verified. Valid CIs combining many model predictions with few gold labels. Applied to LLM eval in **AutoEval Done Right** (Boyeau et al. 2024, arXiv:2403.07008), Stratified PPI (arXiv:2406.04291), Adaptive PP-AutoEval (NeurIPS 2025, arXiv:2505.18659). Relevant as a principled way to combine a prior predictor with 1–31 revealed labels.
59. **[P] Anchor Points** (entry 18), **100 instances** (47), **Active Evaluation Acquisition** (31), **Fluid** (22), **Scales++** (32), **Contextual MIRT** (26), **CPP** (40): the few-shot/cold-start results across these are summarized in the synthesis.
60. **[P] Cold-start collaborative filtering with side information**: Vartak et al., *A Meta-Learning Perspective on Cold-Start Recommendations for Items* (NeurIPS 2017), verified; Volkovs, Yu, Poutanen, *DropoutNet* (NeurIPS 2017), verified; Lee et al., *MeLU* (KDD 2019), arXiv:1908.00413, verified. Items/benchmarks with no interaction history are handled by content features plus meta-learned adaptation from few interactions; directly transferable framing (benchmark = new "item group", subject = user).
61. **[P] Correcting Prompt Dependence in LLM Benchmarks: Bayesian Hierarchical Model with Embedding-Space Clustering** — Llewellyn et al. (CTB@ICML 2026), arXiv:2510.05709. Verified. Partial pooling over embedding clusters of prompts improves MAE 4–73% in limited-data settings; a template for hierarchical shrinkage at n≤31.

## 6. Calibration, proper scoring rules, and learning-curve-area metrics

62. **[S] Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation** — Gneiting & Raftery (JASA 102(477):359–378, 2007). Bibliographic record confirmed; full text not fetched. Brier and log score are strictly proper; implications: predicting calibrated probabilities, not hedged 0.5, is optimal in expectation.
63. **[S] Murphy (1973) Brier decomposition** — Brier = Reliability − Resolution + Uncertainty (secondary sources verified, e.g., arXiv:1303.6182; Siegert 2016 QJRMS simplification). Practical reading for PAIEC: at n=0 the floor is the benchmark's base-rate uncertainty p(1−p); gains come from resolution (separating items/subjects), and miscalibration is paid in full.
64. **[P] On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks** — Guo, Pleiss, Sun, Weinberger (ICML 2017), arXiv:1706.04599. Verified. Defines ECE as used in PAIEC diagnostics; temperature scaling.
65. **[P] Results of the Active Learning Challenge** — Guyon, Cawley, Dror, Lemaire (AISTATS 2010 workshop, PMLR 16:19–45, 2011), https://proceedings.mlr.press/v16/guyon11a.html. Verified. Defines **ALC** as the log-scaled, normalized area under the (AUC-vs-#labels) learning curve, the direct ancestor of PAIEC's Brier-ALC on a log2(1+n) axis. Lesson from that challenge: log-axis weighting makes the first few labels dominate; prior-only (n=0) quality matters a lot.
66. **[P] NeurIPS 2020 Predicting Generalization in Deep Learning (PGDL)** — Jiang et al., arXiv:2012.07976; retrospective PMLR 133 (2021), https://proceedings.mlr.press/v133/jiang21a.html. Verified. A "predict a quantity without running it" competition; retrospective documents failure modes (measures overfitting to the public task set), a caution for benchmark-level cold start.

## 7. Meta-learning / algorithm selection ancestry

67. **[S] The Algorithm Selection Problem** — J. R. Rice (Advances in Computers 15:65–118, 1976). Bibliography from secondary sources only (unverified DOI). Problem space × feature space × algorithm space → performance space; PAIEC is Rice's framework with items as problems, subjects as algorithms, and item_content/subject metadata as features.
68. **[S] Meta-Learning: A Survey** — J. Vanschoren (2018), arXiv:1810.03548. Verified. Meta-features, meta-learners, meta-targets; OpenML as a repository of prior evaluations, the same role measurement-db plays.
69. **[S] Learning Curves for Decision Making in Supervised ML: A Survey** — Mohr & van Rijn (Machine Learning 113, 2024), arXiv:2201.12150. Verified. Taxonomy of learning-curve models and extrapolation; relevant for predicting how the Brier curve itself should fall with n.

## Synthesis

### (a) How predictable is per-item LLM correctness, and how many labels do you need?

- *Aggregate-level* predictability is high: model×task matrices are low-rank (Ye et al. R²>95%; Ruan et al.'s few principal capabilities; Sloth; metabench's single factor r≈0.94). ~100 well-chosen items recover full-benchmark accuracy within ~2% (tinyBenchmarks); with IRT and adaptive selection, 10–50 items match or beat full-benchmark accuracy on validity/variance (Fluid; ATLAS; IRSL "50 questions"). Anchor Points show ~a dozen items can even reconstruct per-example predictions in classification tasks where model confidences are correlated.
- *Per-item* predictability is much lower and strongly model-dependent. With prompt-only features and no history of the target model, assessors on MMLU-Pro are often near random; the best reach AUROC≈0.7 (PredictaBoard). With richer, interpretable demand features (ADeLe) AUROC≈0.84 in-distribution, 0.81 on new tasks, 0.75 on **new benchmarks**, with ECE≈0.01–0.04; plain text-embedding assessors collapse to ≈0.48 on new benchmarks. Correctness is also more predictable for high-accuracy models (PredictaBoard), and human-difficulty is only loosely concordant with model difficulty (Zhou et al. Nature 2024; Lugoloobi et al.).
- *Few-shot adaptation*: the "100 instances" result is that a generic assessor conditioned on ~100 reference outcomes matches a model-specific assessor in-distribution, saturating around 100; random reference selection is as good as clever selection; OOD, everything degrades. PAIEC's budget tops out at 31, so expect to be on the steep part of that curve, where the benchmark-level prior (n=0) and shrinkage dominate, consistent with ALC's log-axis weighting and with the participant repo's finding that a smoothed label mean is already a strong baseline (~0.21 Brier-ALC).
- *Where transferable signal lives*: (i) subject ability from other benchmarks (IRT θ / principal capabilities / model embeddings), which transfers to benchmarks "measuring the same objective" (IRSL, Ruan) but whose concept labels are unreliable guides (Desai et al.); (ii) item difficulty from text, which explains roughly half the reliable variance in educational item banks (Chen & Chen R²≈0.53) and is best captured via demand rubrics rather than raw embeddings for cross-benchmark transfer (ADeLe, Scales++); (iii) a benchmark-level base rate, which is the dominant term of the Brier decomposition at n=0 and the quantity 1–7 labels estimate most efficiently.
- *Calibration*: Brier rewards calibrated hedging; tree models overfit/miscalibrate at small n (PredictaBoard); GCM shows cross-model training yields ECE<0.03 without post-hoc fixes; hierarchical Bayesian shrinkage (Llewellyn et al.; PAIEC participant repo) is the natural way to interpolate between the prior and 31 labels.

### (b) Open questions

1. Benchmark-level cold start is largely untested in the IRT literature: Truong et al. 2025, Fluid, ATLAS, and IRSL all calibrate on the benchmark being evaluated; Contextual MIRT reports failure across scenarios; only ADeLe and Scales++ report genuine new-benchmark results.
2. Which item features transfer across benchmarks? Evidence favours interpretable demand/skill rubrics over raw embeddings, but rubric annotation needs an LLM (allowed via API at scoring time, though web search is prohibited).
3. How to model *subjects* defined by harness and reasoning effort (not just base model): PromptEval-style template effects and IRSL's test-time-scaling results are the only related work.
4. Optimal acquisition under a Brier-ALC objective at n≤31: active-testing theory targets accuracy estimation, not per-item probability; Pacchiardi et al. found random ≈ clever at n=100; whether Fisher-information or uncertainty sampling helps at n=1–7 is open.
5. Item noise ceiling: label errors and grading problems (Fantastic Bugs; Guerdan) bound achievable Brier and are benchmark-specific.
6. Assessor training loss vs target metric (Romero-Alvarado et al.) and the proper-scoring-rule choice for few-shot updating.
